I remember sitting in a lecture hall at Universite Paris Cite in late 2022 when OpenAI released their playground. I opened it on my laptop while the professor was going through slides I'd already read the night before. Within minutes I knew something had shifted — not just in tech, but in what it meant to learn, to build, to work.

I was supposed to follow the path. The French system is clear about it: get into the right school, get the right degree, get the right job. I'd already done a spring at J.P. Morgan. I was doing research at the university. Everything was on track.

But I couldn't unsee what I'd seen.

## The decision

Dropping out in France is not like dropping out in the US. There's no romanticized dropout-to-billionaire narrative here. In France, your degree is your identity. When you tell people you're leaving university, they don't ask "what are you building?" — they ask "what went wrong?"

Nothing went wrong. Everything went right. I saw the future arriving faster than any curriculum could keep up with, and I decided I'd rather be building it than studying about it three years too late.

## What happened next

I co-founded TruePerson in 2023. I shipped product. I broke things. I learned more in six months of real work than in a year of coursework. Not because the coursework was bad — some of it was genuinely good — but because building forces you to confront problems that textbooks carefully avoid.

Since then I've shipped across startups back to back — Stox, Sonic Labs, doublespeed, Trendtrack, and now Rippletide. Each one taught me something different. Each one confirmed the same thing: the best way to learn is to ship.

## Why I'm writing this

I think anyone who wants to hire me should read this to understand. Not because dropping out makes me special — it doesn't. But because it says something about how I make decisions.

I don't optimize for safety. I optimize for learning rate. When I see a higher-slope path, I take it, even when it means leaving a perfectly good one behind. That's what dropping out was. Not a rejection of education, but a bet on a faster one.

I'm not against degrees. I'm against waiting. The world is moving too fast to spend four years preparing for a version of it that won't exist by the time you graduate.

## To anyone considering the same

I won't tell you to drop out. That would be irresponsible. What I will tell you is this: pay attention to the gap between what you're learning and what the world actually needs. If that gap is small, stay. If it's growing every month, ask yourself hard questions.

And whatever you decide — build something. Ship it. Put your name on it. That's the real education.
